Cities, Air Quality and Waste

Air, waste and disaster risk in cities rest on the same data infrastructure; risk that is measured and explained in terms everyone understands turns into action. This section brings together three sessions (K03, K10, K14).

Read this section in the report

What did I hear in the field?

  • Clean air can be told like a product. In the session at the Kazakhstan Pavilion, the Taza initiative (Almaty and Kathmandu) described the effect of translating science into language the public understands; a narrative that shows polluted air as a cigarette equivalent was used. A three-dimensional model at 30-metre resolution was built for Almaty; according to the speakers, domestic coal is a more effective source than a thermal power plant at breathing level.
  • Science, satellites and machine learning work together. CAMS and ECMWF experts described the smoke of the 2021 Manavgat fire and the transport of Saharan dust. The joint Türkiye–South Korea satellite monitoring team presented a 1 km resolution PM10 map made with machine learning and its relationship to mortality.
  • Zero Waste is turning into an ecosystem of practice. Türkiye's experience was presented within the D-8 cooperation framework. Linked initiatives such as the Neighbourhood-Scale Cities Programme, the 1 Billion Challenge (food waste), the Zero Waste Generation and the Global Zero Waste Forum were explained. Türkiye also announced six new initiatives on ocean, marine and waste-sector methane.
  • Examples from other countries. Indonesia aims to manage all of its waste by 2029; Malaysia shared its waste-sector emissions and its single-use plastics roadmap (speaker-reported).
  • In urban resilience, technology is a tool. In the Resilient Cities session it was stressed that AI and data centres must reduce their own energy, water and carbon footprint. It was said that local governments should access finance in proportion to the burden they carry, and that early warning running on uninterrupted connectivity is the infrastructure of urban resilience. Examples were presented from Bursa, BTK and GSMA.

Through the eyes of climate science and meteorological engineering

The amount of particulate matter in the air depends not only on emissions but also on the weather. Wind disperses the pollutant, the mixing height in the lower atmosphere traps it near the ground, and precipitation washes particles out of the air. The study I am running with classmates on this washout effect for PM10 and PM2.5 in Istanbul points in the same direction as the modelling approach described in these sessions. Air quality forecasting comes from combining emission data with meteorological forecasts.

My recommendations as a climate ambassador

  1. Add meteorological parameters to air quality forecasting systematically. Precipitation, wind and lower-atmosphere data should be combined with high-resolution PM10 maps. The results of the Türkiye–South Korea satellite monitoring project should be linked to national early warning.
  2. Explain air quality in language the public understands. If simple indicators such as the cigarette equivalent were used in Turkish in schools and on social media, risk awareness would rise. This would be the link where scientific data meets the citizen.
  3. Take Zero Waste to the neighbourhood scale and to young people. Türkiye can support the call to align NDCs with zero waste targets as an example. University campuses could join the Zero Waste Generation initiative as pilot neighbourhoods.
  4. Let local governments access finance in proportion to their burden. In examples like Bursa, digital twins (air quality, noise, heat island) and cell broadcast early warning pilots should be scaled. Data centres should openly report their energy and water footprint.
  5. Build a university–municipality data partnership. The measurement and modelling capacity of schools such as ITU, together with student projects (such as the Istanbul precipitation–PM10 study and Uray's weather and route data), could supply input to municipal decision-making.
