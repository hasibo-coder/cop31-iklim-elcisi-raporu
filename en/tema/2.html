Energy, Grids and Electrification

The bottleneck of the energy transition is no longer the generation target; it is the grid, the speed of permitting and long-term predictability. This section brings the energy sessions (K20, K21, K22, K25, K27, K28) into a single story.

Read this section in the report

What did I hear in the field?

  • Energy security is a matter of sovereignty. At the energy security roundtable at the Business Forum, the Strait of Hormuz crisis was the shared lesson: the lasting answer is to produce energy rather than import it, along with electrification and the grid. Representatives of Turkish companies described how supplier diversification helped during the crisis (speaker-reported).
  • The investor's first expectation is predictability. Speakers stressed long-term regulatory clarity, such as 15–20 year price or volume guarantees; the UK's CfD and RAB models were given as examples.
  • The real bottleneck is the grid. The pace of electrification is set less by targets than by grid connection and finance for developing countries. The "35-by-35" initiative, which aims to raise the electrification share to 35%, was mentioned in this framework.
  • Distributed renewables are more resilient. A Ukrainian energy representative described how distributed wind installations stayed more resilient under attacks on centralized thermal plants. In Germany the permitting time has fallen to 18 months, and the EU target is 12 months (speaker-reported).
  • The job is not done when the installation is finished. Representatives of Papua New Guinea, Save the Children and Sano Utilities, speaking on school and community solar systems in the Pacific, stressed that maintenance finance, local capacity and women's participation determine how long a facility lasts.
  • Grids are crossing borders. In the ASEAN session, the ASEAN Power Grid (APG) between member countries and the Southeast Asian examples that finance it offered a point of comparison for Türkiye's connections with its neighbours. Climate TRACE, for its part, argued that facility-level open data lets limited finance go to the most effective facilities.

Through the eyes of climate science and meteorological engineering

Wind and solar generate according to the weather, which puts meteorology at the center of grid planning. Daily and hourly generation forecasts, the way heat waves raise the cooling load on the demand side, and the effect of cold spells on heating demand in winter must be read together. An installed capacity target (175 GW for 2040) is not enough on its own. We also need to know at which hour, at which point on the grid and under which weather conditions we will use that capacity. A good forecast reduces the need for storage and the cost of backup plants.

My recommendations as a climate ambassador

  1. Plan grid and permitting times together with the targets. Reaching the 2040 targets depends on the speed of grid connection and permitting. Germany's 18 months and the EU's 12-month target (speaker-reported) could be a reference point for Türkiye.
  2. Make weather forecasting part of grid operation. The Turkish State Meteorological Service (MGM), university meteorology departments and the system operator should work together on wind and solar generation forecasts and on heat-driven demand forecasts. This would also open a new career field for meteorological engineers.
  3. Use weather data in transport electrification. In the Climate TRACE session, the electrification of heavy trucks was mentioned as the next wave. Because conditions such as temperature, wind and gradient affect energy use, weather-aware route optimization systems like Uray can support this transition.
  4. Budget post-installation maintenance from the start. The Pacific example budgeted not only the installation of a project but its whole life-cycle maintenance. In campus projects such as the hybrid solar–wind lighting system we designed at the ITU Ayazağa Pond, a maintenance plan and ownership should be part of the first design.
  5. Address energy security together with domestic generation, storage and demand flexibility. The Hormuz lesson showed the risk of depending on a single source or route. The 2040 storage target and demand-flexibility tools such as smart meters should be a priority from this angle.
