Nature, Ocean, Agriculture and Fire

Nature, ocean and land are parts of the same landscape; measurable, community-based and funded action proves stronger than response after the event. This section brings together five sessions (K08, K09, K11, K30, K31).

Read this section in the report

What did I hear in the field?

  • Ocean observation is an operational necessity. Representatives of the WMO, IOC-UNESCO (GOOS) and ESA said ocean observation is needed for early warning, weather forecasting and for monitoring systems such as El Niño and the AMOC. For coastal adaptation, local projections that include land subsidence (InSAR/GNSS) came to the fore.
  • Observation funding leans on a few countries. 90% of global observation funding comes from nine countries (verified). This is a risk to the sustainability of the observation network.
  • One Landscape, Three Conventions. At the 3rd High-Level Dialogue on Rio Synergy, the COP presidencies of Türkiye, Mongolia and Armenia addressed climate, biodiversity and land degradation together. A voluntary, country-driven group was formally launched, without a new secretariat, funding architecture or additional reporting.
  • In fire, prevention outlasts suppression. In the session under the FAO umbrella, it was stressed that success should be measured by the effectiveness of prevention, not by the number of fires put out. A shared landscape model managed by local cooperatives in Ghana was given as an example.
  • Carbon farming is more than producing credits. At the Italy Pavilion, Tuscia University and certification experts said carbon farming and biochar must be backed by sound MRV, training and the EU CRCF standards.
  • In agricultural finance, training comes first. In the session on climate finance and adaptation in the Brazilian agri-food sector, credit cooperatives, technical training before credit, models with offtake guarantees and first-loss, and insurance premium discounts for sustainable producers came to the fore.

Through the eyes of climate science and meteorological engineering

The ocean is the atmosphere's main source of heat and moisture, so ocean observations are a direct input to weather forecast models. Fire danger, too, is read through meteorological indicators that track temperature, humidity, wind and drought together; linking these indicators to early warning is the scientific basis of prevention. In carbon farming, the precipitation regime and soil moisture affect how durable the stored carbon is. In other words, all three topics are parts of the same data chain.

My recommendations as a climate ambassador

  1. Produce local sea-level projections that include land subsidence for coastal cities. Ground motion measured with InSAR and GNSS should be added to sea-level projections for the Mediterranean and Black Sea coasts. A joint working group of MGM and universities could start this.
  2. Put prevention and community first in forest fires. Fire meteorology and early warning should be combined with training for local cooperatives and village communities. The success indicator should be the effectiveness of prevention; the experience of the 2021 Manavgat fire offers a concrete reference for this lesson.
  3. Turn the Rio Synergy into a single landscape plan in Türkiye. Climate, biodiversity and desertification plans should be monitored with shared indicators and shared data, without creating an additional reporting burden.
  4. Build agricultural finance together with training, cooperatives and insurance. MRV training for carbon farming, clustering models for small producers and insurance premium discounts for sustainable producers (mechanisms such as TARSİM) should be considered together.
  5. Make Türkiye's contribution to the observation network visible. The concentration of observation funding in a few countries makes the network fragile. If MGM and the universities share marine and atmospheric observation data with global systems, it would also raise Türkiye's scientific standing.
