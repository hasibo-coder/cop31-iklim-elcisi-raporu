Industry, Trade and Carbon Markets

In heavy industry the problem is not the appetite to invest but the policy calendar and shared definitions: a clear carbon price, a free-allocation timetable and interoperable markets. This section brings together seven sessions (K04, K12, K13, K17, K18, K24, K26).

Read this section in the report

What did I hear in the field?

  • Industrial decarbonization is moving from pledge to implementation. In the LeadIT 3.0 negotiations it was said that the investment gap in hard-to-abate sectors can be closed with de-risking finance, a carbon price, shared CCS and hydrogen infrastructure, and country platforms. The COP31 Presidency of Türkiye made green industrialization a priority; COP32 will continue it.
  • The Antalya Green Industrialization Accord rests on three pillars. Finance and a project pool, market creation, and cooperation on carbon pricing and carbon leakage. The goal is to raise heavy-industry investment about 25-fold to above USD 500 billion by 2035 (speaker-reported). According to the OECD, the real bottleneck is project preparation.
  • TIDIP is Türkiye's industrial transformation platform. I took part as a climate ambassador in the launch organized by the Ministry of Industry and Technology, the EBRD, the World Bank Group and the European Commission. The platform targets iron and steel, cement, aluminium and fertilizer.
  • According to steel representatives, the calendar matters more than money. In the session that included Erdemir and other producers, what stood out was less a lack of finance than the need for clarity on the 2030 ETS free-allocation calendar, grid emission intensity and non-binding roadmaps. Verifying the facility-level carbon benchmark was also seen as necessary for CBAM compliance. I think this clarity would speed up investment decisions.
  • Carbon markets must talk to each other. At the Open Coalition on Compliance Carbon Markets (OCCCM) meeting, China, the EU and the Republic of Korea, together with others, aimed for Article 6–compatible, high-integrity, interoperable markets. Türkiye stressed that ETS design authority should stay in the country.
  • Trade and regional climate policy are intertwined. At the first Trade and Economy Ministers meeting of the Organization of Turkic States (OTS), climate policy, green finance and digital trade were treated as shared standards; the Antalya Ministerial Declaration was signed. In transport, a dialogue with UITP, ICLEI and Brasília showed that carbon credits need to be planned at the start of a project if they are to close the funding gap of large projects.

Through the eyes of climate science and meteorological engineering

Carbon pricing and CBAM rest on measurement, reporting and verification (MRV), which means scientific data. Comparing facility reports (inventories) with atmospheric measurements, that is, ground and satellite observations, increases reliability. Facility-level open data like Climate TRACE matters in this context, because even two steel plants of the same owner standing side by side can show different emission intensities. Atmospheric science is the outer ring of this verification chain.

My recommendations as a climate ambassador

  1. Announce the 2030 ETS allocation calendar and the CBAM steps early and clearly. The steel sector's message that "the calendar matters more than money" should be read as a constructive call. Verifying the facility-level carbon benchmark should be part of this calendar.
  2. Open the TIDIP project preparation support window to SME suppliers and organized industrial zones (OIZs) as well. The bottleneck the OECD points to is project preparation. Universities such as ITU and student teams can support the technical preparation.
  3. Strengthen national MRV capacity with atmospheric and measurement sciences. A verification team could be set up that compares facility reports with ground and satellite observations; meteorological and environmental engineers would be the natural experts in this field.
  4. Make interoperability a priority in carbon market design. The Türkiye ETS should be designed to be compatible with the EU, China and Korea, and Article 6–compatible. In transport, weather-aware route optimization in road logistics (the area Uray works in) can produce measurable fuel and emission savings; this data could feed into a transport carbon credit methodology.
  5. Tie OTS and D-8 trade cooperation to shared green standards. Shared MRV, green certification and CBAM alignment both strengthen trade between countries and protect firms exporting to the EU.
