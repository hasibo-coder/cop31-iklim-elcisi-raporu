Abstract

This study synthesizes the notes and session reports from the 33 sessions I attended at COP31 Antalya as an ITU Climate Ambassador. The sessions are grouped into seven themes: climate finance; energy, grids and electrification; industry, trade and carbon markets; nature, ocean, agriculture and fire; cities, air quality and waste; youth, children and education; and science, diplomacy and ministerial sessions. For each theme I present the views heard in the field, the figures, and an assessment from a climate science and meteorological engineering perspective, followed by recommendations. Figures that I could not verify against an independent source are marked as "speaker-reported". The findings are brought together in eight implications for Türkiye and a set of consolidated recommendations arranged by theme. This is not an official summary of COP31; it is limited to the sessions I attended.

Keywords: COP31, climate finance, energy transition, carbon markets, climate diplomacy, Türkiye

1. Introduction and Method

This report gathers the notes from the 33 sessions I attended at COP31 Antalya as an ITU Climate Ambassador into seven themes and links them to implications for Türkiye. Its readers are the general public, university students and social media followers; for that reason the language is plain, the narrative flows, and the figures have been checked.

For each session I first prepared a summary card: speakers, main message, figures, decisions and the Türkiye connection. I then distributed the cards across the seven themes. Instead of describing sessions one by one, each section builds a shared story.

The "speaker-reported" label. I was able to verify some of the figures against an independent source; others are simply values the speaker stated on stage. Where I could not verify a figure, I wrote "according to the speaker" and did not present it as hard data. The verification summary is in the appendix at the end of the report.

Scope and limits. The report is limited to the sessions I attended; it is not an official summary of the whole of COP31. Session content rests on my notes and the session reports. The interpretations are mine and are not presented as the speakers' views.

— Hasan İbrahim Bulduk

2. Findings

This section presents the findings from the sessions under seven themes. Each theme follows the same order: views heard in the field, a figures box, an assessment from a climate science and meteorological engineering perspective, and recommendations.

2.1 Theme 1 – Climate Finance and Capital

The capital exists; what is missing is the bridge that connects it to long-term, predictable and investment-ready projects. This section collects the shared message I heard in the finance sessions (K01, K16, K23, K33; and secondarily K18 and K24).

What did I hear in the field?

  • Banking now works with climate. According to Garanti BBVA representatives, sustainable finance has spread from energy to heavy industry such as steel, cement and fertilizer. A company applying for credit is expected to provide climate data and a science-based decarbonization plan.
  • The real obstacle is not money but maturity and uncertainty. High inflation and commercial maturities of 2–3 years make long-horizon investment difficult. Blended finance with development banks and long-term loans with grace periods came forward as solutions.
  • Climate risk is a systemic financial risk. At the asset owners' roundtable, risk was described as non-linear, cumulative and impossible to reduce through diversification. Scenarios were said to underprice tipping points. What slows flows to emerging markets, in turn, is the perceived risk premium.
  • Capital is ready; the project pipeline is not ready enough. The Unlocking Capital session made three proposals: project preparation, recording climate action in institutional accounting, and open-source climate data.
  • Public money draws private capital into risky areas. In the Brazil Climate Fund and KfW example, blended finance mobilized private capital for risky work such as forest restoration. In Türkiye the same logic works in TIDIP (EBRD, the World Bank and the Turkish government); I cover it under Theme 3.

Through the eyes of a climate science and meteorological engineering student

Banks are asking for "climate data"; whether this data is reliable depends on the observation–model–forecast triad behind it. Physical risks such as drought, flood, extreme heat and storms change over the life of a loan. Historical averages are therefore not enough; forward-looking, high-resolution climate projections are needed. That scenarios underprice tipping points is a warning that should be taken seriously from a scientific point of view as well.

My recommendations as a climate ambassador

  1. Build a shared data bridge for physical risk. Set out an open methodology for the climate data banks use in credit assessment. Universities with meteorology and climate departments (such as ITU) should take a validating role.
  2. Give project preparation support to young people and early-stage ventures. The capital exists, but there are few ready projects. For ventures such as Uray, which does weather-aware route optimization, showing measurable fuel and emission savings could be the first step to accessing finance.
  3. Solve the maturity mismatch for small investors too. Loan products with grace periods and 6–10 year maturities should be supported by capital market instruments open not only to large industry but also to SMEs and small investors.
  4. Test the return on adaptation investment with Türkiye data. The claim of "about USD 10 returned for every USD 1 of adaptation investment" is speaker-reported and its source could not be verified. An independent Türkiye study should test this claim.
  5. Expand open facility-level and climate data. Open facility-level data like Climate TRACE should be made available to banks and researchers with the same transparency for facilities in Türkiye.

2.2 Theme 2 – Energy, Grids and Electrification

The bottleneck of the energy transition is no longer the generation target; it is the grid, the speed of permitting and long-term predictability. This section brings the energy sessions (K20, K21, K22, K25, K27, K28) into a single story.

What did I hear in the field?

  • Energy security is a matter of sovereignty. At the energy security roundtable at the Business Forum, the Strait of Hormuz crisis was the shared lesson: the lasting answer is to produce energy rather than import it, along with electrification and the grid. Representatives of Turkish companies described how supplier diversification helped during the crisis (speaker-reported).
  • The investor's first expectation is predictability. Speakers stressed long-term regulatory clarity, such as 15–20 year price or volume guarantees; the UK's CfD and RAB models were given as examples.
  • The real bottleneck is the grid. The pace of electrification is set less by targets than by grid connection and finance for developing countries. The "35-by-35" initiative, which aims to raise the electrification share to 35%, was mentioned in this framework.
  • Distributed renewables are more resilient. A Ukrainian energy representative described how distributed wind installations stayed more resilient under attacks on centralized thermal plants. In Germany the permitting time has fallen to 18 months, and the EU target is 12 months (speaker-reported).
  • The job is not done when the installation is finished. Representatives of Papua New Guinea, Save the Children and Sano Utilities, speaking on school and community solar systems in the Pacific, stressed that maintenance finance, local capacity and women's participation determine how long a facility lasts.
  • Grids are crossing borders. In the ASEAN session, the ASEAN Power Grid (APG) between member countries and the Southeast Asian examples that finance it offered a point of comparison for Türkiye's connections with its neighbours. Climate TRACE, for its part, argued that facility-level open data lets limited finance go to the most effective facilities.

Through the eyes of a climate science and meteorological engineering student

Wind and solar generate according to the weather, which puts meteorology at the center of grid planning. Daily and hourly generation forecasts, the way heat waves raise the cooling load on the demand side, and the effect of cold spells on heating demand in winter must be read together. An installed capacity target (175 GW for 2040) is not enough on its own. We also need to know at which hour, at which point on the grid and under which weather conditions we will use that capacity. A good forecast reduces the need for storage and the cost of backup plants.

My recommendations as a climate ambassador

  1. Plan grid and permitting times together with the targets. Reaching the 2040 targets depends on the speed of grid connection and permitting. Germany's 18 months and the EU's 12-month target (speaker-reported) could be a reference point for Türkiye.
  2. Make weather forecasting part of grid operation. The Turkish State Meteorological Service (MGM), university meteorology departments and the system operator should work together on wind and solar generation forecasts and on heat-driven demand forecasts. This would also open a new career field for meteorological engineers.
  3. Use weather data in transport electrification. In the Climate TRACE session, the electrification of heavy trucks was mentioned as the next wave. Because conditions such as temperature, wind and gradient affect energy use, weather-aware route optimization systems like Uray can support this transition.
  4. Budget post-installation maintenance from the start. The Pacific example budgeted not only the installation of a project but its whole life-cycle maintenance. In campus projects such as the hybrid solar–wind lighting system we designed at the ITU Ayazağa Pond, a maintenance plan and ownership should be part of the first design.
  5. Address energy security together with domestic generation, storage and demand flexibility. The Hormuz lesson showed the risk of depending on a single source or route. The 2040 storage target and demand-flexibility tools such as smart meters should be a priority from this angle.

2.3 Theme 3 – Industry, Trade and Carbon Markets

In heavy industry the problem is not the appetite to invest but the policy calendar and shared definitions: a clear carbon price, a free-allocation timetable and interoperable markets. This section brings together seven sessions (K04, K12, K13, K17, K18, K24, K26).

What did I hear in the field?

  • Industrial decarbonization is moving from pledge to implementation. In the LeadIT 3.0 negotiations it was said that the investment gap in hard-to-abate sectors can be closed with de-risking finance, a carbon price, shared CCS and hydrogen infrastructure, and country platforms. The COP31 Presidency of Türkiye made green industrialization a priority; COP32 will continue it.
  • The Antalya Green Industrialization Accord rests on three pillars. Finance and a project pool, market creation, and cooperation on carbon pricing and carbon leakage. The goal is to raise heavy-industry investment about 25-fold to above USD 500 billion by 2035 (speaker-reported). According to the OECD, the real bottleneck is project preparation.
  • TIDIP is Türkiye's industrial transformation platform. I took part as a climate ambassador in the launch organized by the Ministry of Industry and Technology, the EBRD, the World Bank Group and the European Commission. The platform targets iron and steel, cement, aluminium and fertilizer.
  • According to steel representatives, the calendar matters more than money. In the session that included Erdemir and other producers, what stood out was less a lack of finance than the need for clarity on the 2030 ETS free-allocation calendar, grid emission intensity and non-binding roadmaps. Verifying the facility-level carbon benchmark was also seen as necessary for CBAM compliance. I think this clarity would speed up investment decisions.
  • Carbon markets must talk to each other. At the Open Coalition on Compliance Carbon Markets (OCCCM) meeting, China, the EU and the Republic of Korea, together with others, aimed for Article 6–compatible, high-integrity, interoperable markets. Türkiye stressed that ETS design authority should stay in the country.
  • Trade and regional climate policy are intertwined. At the first Trade and Economy Ministers meeting of the Organization of Turkic States (OTS), climate policy, green finance and digital trade were treated as shared standards; the Antalya Ministerial Declaration was signed. In transport, a dialogue with UITP, ICLEI and Brasília showed that carbon credits need to be planned at the start of a project if they are to close the funding gap of large projects.

Through the eyes of a climate science and meteorological engineering student

Carbon pricing and CBAM rest on measurement, reporting and verification (MRV), which means scientific data. Comparing facility reports (inventories) with atmospheric measurements, that is, ground and satellite observations, increases reliability. Facility-level open data like Climate TRACE matters in this context, because even two steel plants of the same owner standing side by side can show different emission intensities. Atmospheric science is the outer ring of this verification chain.

My recommendations as a climate ambassador

  1. Announce the 2030 ETS allocation calendar and the CBAM steps early and clearly. The steel sector's message that "the calendar matters more than money" should be read as a constructive call. Verifying the facility-level carbon benchmark should be part of this calendar.
  2. Open the TIDIP project preparation support window to SME suppliers and organized industrial zones (OIZs) as well. The bottleneck the OECD points to is project preparation. Universities such as ITU and student teams can support the technical preparation.
  3. Strengthen national MRV capacity with atmospheric and measurement sciences. A verification team could be set up that compares facility reports with ground and satellite observations; meteorological and environmental engineers would be the natural experts in this field.
  4. Make interoperability a priority in carbon market design. The Türkiye ETS should be designed to be compatible with the EU, China and Korea, and Article 6–compatible. In transport, weather-aware route optimization in road logistics (the area Uray works in) can produce measurable fuel and emission savings; this data could feed into a transport carbon credit methodology.
  5. Tie OTS and D-8 trade cooperation to shared green standards. Shared MRV, green certification and CBAM alignment both strengthen trade between countries and protect firms exporting to the EU.

2.4 Theme 4 – Nature, Ocean, Agriculture and Fire

Nature, ocean and land are parts of the same landscape; measurable, community-based and funded action proves stronger than response after the event. This section brings together five sessions (K08, K09, K11, K30, K31).

What did I hear in the field?

  • Ocean observation is an operational necessity. Representatives of the WMO, IOC-UNESCO (GOOS) and ESA said ocean observation is needed for early warning, weather forecasting and for monitoring systems such as El Niño and the AMOC. For coastal adaptation, local projections that include land subsidence (InSAR/GNSS) came to the fore.
  • Observation funding leans on a few countries. 90% of global observation funding comes from nine countries (verified). This is a risk to the sustainability of the observation network.
  • One Landscape, Three Conventions. At the 3rd High-Level Dialogue on Rio Synergy, the COP presidencies of Türkiye, Mongolia and Armenia addressed climate, biodiversity and land degradation together. A voluntary, country-driven group was formally launched, without a new secretariat, funding architecture or additional reporting.
  • In fire, prevention outlasts suppression. In the session under the FAO umbrella, it was stressed that success should be measured by the effectiveness of prevention, not by the number of fires put out. A shared landscape model managed by local cooperatives in Ghana was given as an example.
  • Carbon farming is more than producing credits. At the Italy Pavilion, Tuscia University and certification experts said carbon farming and biochar must be backed by sound MRV, training and the EU CRCF standards.
  • In agricultural finance, training comes first. In the session on climate finance and adaptation in the Brazilian agri-food sector, credit cooperatives, technical training before credit, models with offtake guarantees and first-loss, and insurance premium discounts for sustainable producers came to the fore.

Through the eyes of a climate science and meteorological engineering student

The ocean is the atmosphere's main source of heat and moisture, so ocean observations are a direct input to weather forecast models. Fire danger, too, is read through meteorological indicators that track temperature, humidity, wind and drought together; linking these indicators to early warning is the scientific basis of prevention. In carbon farming, the precipitation regime and soil moisture affect how durable the stored carbon is. In other words, all three topics are parts of the same data chain.

My recommendations as a climate ambassador

  1. Produce local sea-level projections that include land subsidence for coastal cities. Ground motion measured with InSAR and GNSS should be added to sea-level projections for the Mediterranean and Black Sea coasts. A joint working group of MGM and universities could start this.
  2. Put prevention and community first in forest fires. Fire meteorology and early warning should be combined with training for local cooperatives and village communities. The success indicator should be the effectiveness of prevention; the experience of the 2021 Manavgat fire offers a concrete reference for this lesson.
  3. Turn the Rio Synergy into a single landscape plan in Türkiye. Climate, biodiversity and desertification plans should be monitored with shared indicators and shared data, without creating an additional reporting burden.
  4. Build agricultural finance together with training, cooperatives and insurance. MRV training for carbon farming, clustering models for small producers and insurance premium discounts for sustainable producers (mechanisms such as TARSİM) should be considered together.
  5. Make Türkiye's contribution to the observation network visible. The concentration of observation funding in a few countries makes the network fragile. If MGM and the universities share marine and atmospheric observation data with global systems, it would also raise Türkiye's scientific standing.

2.5 Theme 5 – Cities, Air Quality and Waste

Air, waste and disaster risk in cities rest on the same data infrastructure; risk that is measured and explained in terms everyone understands turns into action. This section brings together three sessions (K03, K10, K14).

What did I hear in the field?

  • Clean air can be told like a product. In the session at the Kazakhstan Pavilion, the Taza initiative (Almaty and Kathmandu) described the effect of translating science into language the public understands; a narrative that shows polluted air as a cigarette equivalent was used. A three-dimensional model at 30-metre resolution was built for Almaty; according to the speakers, domestic coal is a more effective source than a thermal power plant at breathing level.
  • Science, satellites and machine learning work together. CAMS and ECMWF experts described the smoke of the 2021 Manavgat fire and the transport of Saharan dust. The joint Türkiye–South Korea satellite monitoring team presented a 1 km resolution PM10 map made with machine learning and its relationship to mortality.
  • Zero Waste is turning into an ecosystem of practice. Türkiye's experience was presented within the D-8 cooperation framework. Linked initiatives such as the Neighbourhood-Scale Cities Programme, the 1 Billion Challenge (food waste), the Zero Waste Generation and the Global Zero Waste Forum were explained. Türkiye also announced six new initiatives on ocean, marine and waste-sector methane.
  • Examples from other countries. Indonesia aims to manage all of its waste by 2029; Malaysia shared its waste-sector emissions and its single-use plastics roadmap (speaker-reported).
  • In urban resilience, technology is a tool. In the Resilient Cities session it was stressed that AI and data centres must reduce their own energy, water and carbon footprint. It was said that local governments should access finance in proportion to the burden they carry, and that early warning running on uninterrupted connectivity is the infrastructure of urban resilience. Examples were presented from Bursa, BTK and GSMA.

Through the eyes of a climate science and meteorological engineering student

The amount of particulate matter in the air depends not only on emissions but also on the weather. Wind disperses the pollutant, the mixing height in the lower atmosphere traps it near the ground, and precipitation washes particles out of the air. The study I am running with classmates on this washout effect for PM10 and PM2.5 in Istanbul points in the same direction as the modelling approach described in these sessions. Air quality forecasting comes from combining emission data with meteorological forecasts.

My recommendations as a climate ambassador

  1. Add meteorological parameters to air quality forecasting systematically. Precipitation, wind and lower-atmosphere data should be combined with high-resolution PM10 maps. The results of the Türkiye–South Korea satellite monitoring project should be linked to national early warning.
  2. Explain air quality in language the public understands. If simple indicators such as the cigarette equivalent were used in Turkish in schools and on social media, risk awareness would rise. This would be the link where scientific data meets the citizen.
  3. Take Zero Waste to the neighbourhood scale and to young people. Türkiye can support the call to align NDCs with zero waste targets as an example. University campuses could join the Zero Waste Generation initiative as pilot neighbourhoods.
  4. Let local governments access finance in proportion to their burden. In examples like Bursa, digital twins (air quality, noise, heat island) and cell broadcast early warning pilots should be scaled. Data centres should openly report their energy and water footprint.
  5. Build a university–municipality data partnership. The measurement and modelling capacity of schools such as ITU, together with student projects (such as the Istanbul precipitation–PM10 study and Uray's weather and route data), could supply input to municipal decision-making.

2.6 Theme 6 – Youth, Children and Education

In climate action, young people are not only listeners; once channels for education, skills and finance open, they are partners with a seat at the decision table. This section brings together four sessions (K02, K07, K29, K32); I attended most of them both as a listener and as a young climate ambassador.

What did I hear in the field?

  • Ocean education is turning into community. In the session at the SDG Pavilion, young entrepreneurs and Blue Planet Alliance representatives turned ocean and climate learning into local action. Campaigns against deep-sea mining and examples of seaweed-based biodegradable fabric were mentioned.
  • Understanding eco-anxiety rather than suppressing it. The "Strong Youth in Climate" workshop at the Türkiye Children and Youth Pavilion brought forward emotion exploration, digital climate literacy and collective efficacy through the distinction between what can be controlled, what can be influenced and what is outside control. Participants committed to taking a small step within 72 hours.
  • Youth organizations should build self-sustaining models. Examples from South Asia (commercial afforestation with corporate responsibility in Bangladesh, composting and rooftop farming in schools, vocational green skills in a UNESCO club) showed that awareness can turn into economic and political action.
  • Green skills should be a policy indicator. The call was for green skills to be an indicator in national education policy and in NDC reports, and for the private sector to support green employment.
  • Children's voices should be at the centre. Child rights advocates stressed child-focused climate data lines, fair finance and safe participation in countries. It was said that in data negotiations the humane purpose of the data should not be forgotten amid technical detail.

Through the eyes of a climate science and meteorological engineering student

When teaching young people climate literacy, the first thing to distinguish is weather from climate: weather is measured in days, climate in decades. The second critical skill is understanding uncertainty; knowing that a forecast expresses a probability and that scenarios show a range rather than a single future reduces both eco-anxiety and misinformation. The extreme heat and unverified news simulations in the workshop are a strong approach in this respect.

My recommendations as a climate ambassador

  1. Take weather and climate literacy into schools. University meteorology and environment clubs (for example ITU METAR) could run short workshops in high schools on the weather–climate difference, uncertainty and reliable data sources.
  2. Make green skills an indicator in national education policy and the NDC. Opening internship and vocational training pathways in fields such as meteorology, energy and water would grow youth employment and climate action together.
  3. Turn eco-anxiety into an action channel. The "three areas" approach (control/influence/outside control) and the small-step commitment could become a standard model at campus and school events.
  4. Open small direct finance channels to schools and youth ventures. Student ventures like Uray are also examples that can be visible on platforms such as COP. Project preparation support (Theme 1) should be combined with a window specifically for young entrepreneurs.
  5. Spread peer teaching and the master–apprentice model. In university preparation, dormitory and club settings, a structure close to the Ahi tradition, in which an experienced student teaches a new one, could be supported with AI-assisted resources. Child-focused data lines should feed into this model as well.

2.7 Theme 7 – Science, Diplomacy and Ministerial Sessions

In the negotiation rooms the shared language is science; in Antalya this language turned into regional cooperation and bridges to implementation. This section brings together four sessions (K05, K06, K15, K19).

What did I hear in the field?

  • The D-8 ministerial session, from Istanbul to Antalya. The session, attended by COP31 President Murat Kurum, aimed to turn the 17 July 2026 Istanbul Declaration into action. The Antalya Ministerial Action Framework, the 2027–2030 D-8 Climate and Environment Roadmap and a D-8–specific Zero Waste Roadmap came onto the agenda. The Climate Implementation Bridge matches national priorities with projects and finance without imposing targets. The Presidency also proposed a humanitarian "COP31 Antalya Gaza Climate Line" (water purification, solar-battery units, shelter).
  • Culture and indigenous knowledge enter adaptation plans. At the meeting of the Group of Friends of Culture-Based Climate Action, including cultural heritage and indigenous knowledge in National Adaptation Plans and climate finance was framed as a move "from recognition to integration". The Global Goal on Adaptation includes five cultural heritage indicators.
  • The Turkic states see water and mountain risk as shared. At the OTS Environment and Climate Ministers roundtable, the Aral Sea, water scarcity and glacier melt were presented as shared regional threats. The priority is to turn national climate plans into bankable projects; the 2027 "Year of Nature and Environmental Protection", a Central Asia climate centre in Uzbekistan and a Global Mountain Summit in Kyrgyzstan are the calendar for this.
  • Science: overshoot must not become the new normal. In the session run jointly by Australia and the Presidency, IPCC representatives, Pacific small island states and least developed countries argued for keeping the duration and magnitude of any overshoot of 1.5 °C low. It was said that coral reefs cannot be protected above 1.5 °C and that the Mitigation Work Programme must continue to 2031 with concrete indicators.

Through the eyes of a climate science and meteorological engineering student

The 1.5 °C limit is defined not by the temperature of a single year but by a long-term average; natural fluctuations such as El Niño can push single-year values above the limit. This distinction matters when explaining it to the public: exceeding it in one year does not mean the limit has been crossed, but the trend is a serious warning. In the session it was stressed that the return after an overshoot may not be symmetric and that systems such as coral reefs may suffer permanent damage; scientifically, this is a warning against the assumption that "we can return after the limit".

My recommendations as a climate ambassador

  1. Publish a simple, traceable roadmap from the COP31 outcomes to COP32. The Antalya Accord, the D-8 roadmap and the Zero Waste initiatives should be shared publicly on a single page with indicators and dates.
  2. Build a shared climate information infrastructure for the OTS and D-8. Joint satellite monitoring, a glacier and water observation network, early warning and data sharing, with the participation of MGM and university meteorology departments, could respond to regional risks such as the Aral Sea.
  3. Turn national climate plans into bankable projects. The spirit of the Climate Implementation Bridge should be combined, in Türkiye too, with a project preparation unit (Themes 1 and 3).
  4. Make Turkish scientists and young researchers more visible in the science–policy interface. Contributions from Türkiye as authors and reviewers to the climate services and regional dynamics chapters of the IPCC AR7 process mentioned in the session should increase; observer and internship pathways should be opened for students.
  5. Bring cultural heritage into adaptation plans with indicators. Türkiye's digitization experience and the examples of solar-powered archaeological sites could be carried into National Adaptation Plan indicators.

3. Discussion: Implications for Türkiye and Consolidated Recommendations

Türkiye entered COP31 from a position of strength as host; the next test is whether the frameworks set up in Antalya turn into implementation through a calendar, a grid and data. Below are the eight Türkiye connections I met most often across the seven themes.

Eight implications

  1. TIDIP. The platform launched by the Ministry of Industry and Technology, the EBRD, the World Bank Group and the European Commission ties the transformation of heavy industry to finance, with a target of EUR 5 billion+ in investment (verified). The real test is the speed of project preparation.
  2. The Antalya Green Industrialization Accord. The framework that carries Antalya's name could create new opportunities for Turkish industrialists through tools such as finance, market creation and green public procurement.
  3. The 2030 ETS allocation calendar. According to sector representatives, what actually slows investment is not finance but the uncertainty of the calendar. Clarity would speed up investment decisions.
  4. CBAM. The expansion of its scope and the verification of facility-level emission benchmarks directly affect exporting firms.
  5. The 2040 energy targets. 175 GW of wind and solar, 140 GWh of storage, 10 GW of nuclear (verified). The bottleneck in front of them is the grid and permitting times.
  6. Zero waste. The rise of the recovery rate from 13% to 37.53% (verified) puts Türkiye in the position of a global example; now it is the turn of NDC alignment and the neighbourhood scale.
  7. The Hormuz lesson. Energy security has become a matter of national sovereignty; domestic generation, storage and demand flexibility are parts of the same package of solutions.
  8. OTS and D-8. Climate cooperation gets stronger when regional diplomacy rests on concrete topics such as water, glaciers and joint satellite monitoring.
